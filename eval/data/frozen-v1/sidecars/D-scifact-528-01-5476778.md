# Autoimmunity due to molecular mimicry as a cause of neurological disease

SciFact corpus doc_id: 5476778

Format: released abstract text, not an original paper PDF. Sentence indices are zero-based.

[0] One hypothesis that couples infection with autoimmune disease is molecular mimicry.

[1] Molecular mimicry is characterized by an immune response to an environmental agent that cross-reacts with a host antigen, resulting in disease.

[2] This hypothesis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetes, lupus and multiple sclerosis (MS).

[3] There is limited direct evidence linking causative agents with pathogenic immune reactions in these diseases.

[4] Our study establishes a clear link between viral infection, autoimmunity and neurological disease in humans.

[5] As a model for molecular mimicry, we studied patients with human T-lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV-1)-associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP), a disease that can be indistinguishable from MS (refs.

[6] 5,6,7).

[7] HAM/TSP patients develop antibodies to neurons.

[8] We hypothesized these antibodies would identify a central nervous system (CNS) autoantigen.

[9] Immunoglobulin G isolated from HAM/TSP patients identified heterogeneous nuclear ribonuclear protein-A1 (hnRNP-A1) as the autoantigen.

[10] Antibodies to hnRNP-A1 cross-reacted with HTLV-1-tax, the immune response to which is associated with HAM/TSP (refs.

[11] 5,9).

[12] Immunoglobulin G specifically stained human Betz cells, whose axons are preferentially damaged.

[13] Infusion of autoantibodies in brain sections inhibited neuronal firing, indicative of their pathogenic nature.

[14] These data demonstrate the importance of molecular mimicry between an infecting agent and hnRNP-A1 in autoimmune disease of the CNS.
